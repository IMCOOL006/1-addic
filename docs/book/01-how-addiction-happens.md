# Ch 1 — How Addiction Happens

Source: epub `index_split_007`. Part 1. Paraphrased notes.

## Purpose

Explain how use turns into addiction. The user identifies their own symptoms and risk factors, and gets guidance on finding professional help.

## Key concepts

- **Three reasons people start:** (1) it feels good, (2) it relieves pain or bad feelings ("self-medicating"), (3) it boosts performance (studying, sports, long hours).
  - Feeling good or performing better = **positive reinforcement**. Relieving something unpleasant = **negative reinforcement**.
- **Expectancies:** at first it reliably delivers the expected effect, and that keeps the pattern going.
- **The transition:** **tolerance** (you need more) and **withdrawal** (you feel bad when it wears off) mark the move from social or recreational use to problem use. Eventually you use "just to feel normal."
- **Addiction:** the need to use overrides most other things you value. Use continues despite the harm. (The 12-step "insanity" framing: doing the same thing and expecting different results.)
- **Moderation fantasy:** wishing to be someone who can "take it or leave it" is normal. For people with addiction, quitting entirely is the least risky path, at least for now.
- **Risk factors:** genetics accounts for roughly **40–60%** of vulnerability. Genetic risk is usually *activated* by environment (chaotic home, abuse, a role model who used, peers who used, depression or anxiety). Even with few risk factors you can still develop addiction.
- **Privacy laws:** clinicians can't share what you tell them about substance use without your permission. The exceptions are a risk of harm to yourself or others, and abuse.

## Case story

- **Charlie (23, CS student):** Adderall borrowed from a friend for finals, then habitual use for studying, then insomnia, then sleeping pills at double or triple the dose. He isolates and his grades slip. He steals checks from his mother. ## Exercises

### Ex 1.1 — Addiction Signs and Symptoms
- **Type:** multi-select checklist ("in the past 12 months"), grouped into categories.
- **Categories (items paraphrased):**
  - *Tolerance:* need more for the same effect; the same amount does less.
  - *Withdrawal:* physically or emotionally uncomfortable without it; used something to ease coming down.
  - *Craving:* think about it often; hard to get it out of your head until you act on it.
  - *Loss of control:* broke your own limits; failed to cut back or quit; used more or longer than planned; can't fit in or feel good without it; used when upset or angry; blackouts; overdose; used unknown drugs; anxious about running out.
  - *Legal problems:* arrests or legal trouble; stole to pay for it.
  - *Social or occupational problems:* mistakes at work or school; hurt relationships; failed role obligations (chores, money, caregiving); lost interest in hobbies or people.
  - *Persistent use despite consequences.*
  - *Impairment or distress:* poor self-care; caused or worsened psychological or medical problems.
- **Interpretation:** any item beyond tolerance and withdrawal means at least alcohol- or drug-*related problems*. This is **not a diagnosis**. If unsure, get evaluated by a professional with addiction expertise.
- **Reuse:** the book says to come back to this list whenever you need to strengthen your motivation.
- **App mapping:** checklist screen. Save the result and surface the user's checked items later as "what I'm leaving behind" on the Reasons card or in the SOS flow. Avoid any score or diagnosis language.

### Ex 1.2 — Personal Risk Factors
- **Type:** multi-select checklist.
- **Items (paraphrased):** family member with addiction; depression, anxiety, PTSD or other psychological problems; parents or role models who used heavily; parents or role models who were consistently criminal; teenage peers who used; trouble in school (learning disability, grades); trouble fitting in or making friends; chaos or conflict at home; physical or sexual abuse; started as a child or young teen; smoked or injected drugs; trauma before heavy use.
- **Interpretation:** more checks means more vulnerability, which helps explain "why me." The factors aren't equally weighted (genetics is the biggest).
- **App mapping:** private, sensitive checklist. Default it to locked or hidden. Use it for self-understanding, not scoring. Trauma items should come with a gentle content note and a resources link.

## Non-exercise tools in this chapter

- **"Finding the Words": three elements for asking a professional for help.** (1) Say how hard this is to talk about, which invites empathy. (2) State your concern briefly and directly, and add detail only when asked. (3) Make a direct request (a referral, confidentiality).
  - **App mapping:** a "Reach out" script builder (template plus fill-ins). It's reused for friends and family in Ch 4.
- **Questions to ask a treatment program** (table). Paraphrased, with the answers to look for:
  1. Is it evidence-based? (Behavioral therapy plus medication is ideal.)
  2. Which behavioral therapies? (CBT, motivational interviewing, motivational incentives.)
  3. Do you prescribe medication? (Naltrexone or Vivitrol, acamprosate, methadone, buprenorphine/Suboxone.)
  4. What range of services? (Psychiatric care, medical detox, family therapy, vocational help, self-help groups, social services.)
  5. What continuing care or aftercare is there?
  6. Is there drug or alcohol monitoring? (Testing. Relapse means adjusting the plan, not failure.)
  7. How long is it? (Usually at least 3 months. Longer is better.)
  8. Is 12-step involvement part of it?
  - **App mapping:** "Choosing a program" checklist and notes card in the Resources area.

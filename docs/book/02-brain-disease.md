# Ch 2 — Addiction Is a Brain Disease

Source: epub `index_split_008`. Part 1. Paraphrased notes. Psychoeducation only, no fill-in exercises.

## Purpose

Explain what alcohol and drugs do to the brain, why that undermines rational choice, how addictive behavior is *learned*, and how treatment (therapy plus medication) helps the brain heal. The message is hope: it is a serious disease, and it is very treatable.

## Key concepts

- **The mind on drugs isn't rational.** Early use is a choice, but as addiction progresses the ability to choose rationally gets impaired. The book counters "moral failing" stigma here.
  - *Jody's story:* a non-addicted person weighs going to a party the night before a big meeting. She thinks through pros and cons and makes a plan (leave early, have one drink, skip the pot). That's a "rational brain" decision.
- **Dopamine and the reward system.** Natural rewards (food, music, sex) release dopamine. Drugs and alcohol mimic it or block its recycling, releasing **2–10×** more. That produces euphoria and a strong drive to repeat.
- **Depletion leads to tolerance and anhedonia.** Repeated use depletes dopamine. The drug works less, *and so does everything else*. Loss of pleasure feeds depression. "Chasing the first high" can never be satisfied.
- **Long-term changes.** The frontal lobe (planning, impulse control) is impaired. Glutamate (learning) is altered. Using becomes habit or reflex, especially around people and places linked to use.
- **Learned behavior: Pavlov.** People, places, things and **emotions** become conditioned cues. Cues trigger anticipation, and anticipation is felt as a craving. Craving is a *conditioned response*.
- **Retraining the brain.** Cue reactions are automatic, like reflexes from the "primitive" brain. Recovery means training the frontal lobe to **tune in** to the craving, **have an inner dialogue**, and **choose a response** instead of reacting.
- **Addicted brain vs. rational brain.** Named here and used for the rest of the book. Key metaphor.
- **How treatment works.** You can't erase cue memories, but you can learn *new responses* while the brain heals. The longer you stay abstinent, the better your odds.
  - **CBT** (also called relapse prevention): understand the conditioning, identify triggers, learn coping responses. It's the best-studied therapy for addiction (stimulants, cannabis, alcohol, opioids).
  - **Motivational techniques:** resolve ambivalence. Especially effective combined with CBT.
  - **Mindfulness-based relapse prevention:** (1) be present using the breath, (2) accept experience without judgment.
- **Medications** work in one of three ways: reduce cravings, block the high, or replace the drug. They are compatible with the workbook's skills, and for opioid addiction the book calls them crucial.
- **The brain heals.** Imaging studies show dopamine capacity recovering over about 14 months of abstinence (methamphetamine). CBT improved impulse control and changed activity in self-control brain regions. Pleasure from ordinary things can come back.

## Reference tables (paraphrased)

**How brain changes show up as behavior**

| Brain change | What it does to behavior |
|---|---|
| Huge dopamine release | Euphoria, a strong drive to repeat |
| Lower overall dopamine (with repeated use) | Can't enjoy the drug *or* normal activities |
| Altered glutamate (learning) | Memory, concentration and reasoning suffer |
| Frontal lobe changes | Harder to resist impulses and plan |
| HPA-axis (stress system) changes | Harder to cope with stress; more likely to use under stress |

**FDA-approved medications (as listed in the book)**

| Medication | Used for | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Naltrexone (oral Revia; injectable Vivitrol) | Alcohol, opioids | Blocks pleasurable effects, reduces cravings |
| Acamprosate (Campral) | Alcohol | Reduces cravings |
| Disulfiram | Alcohol | Makes you feel ill if you drink |
| Methadone | Opioids | Detox and relapse prevention; reduces withdrawal and cravings |
| Buprenorphine (sublingual) | Opioids | Detox and relapse prevention; reduces cravings, blocks euphoria |

The book also mentions off-label or investigational drugs (topiramate, ondansetron, quetiapine, baclofen) and points to NIDA (drugabuse.gov). *The app must present medication information as educational only, and should date-stamp it or link to an authoritative live source, because this changes over time.*

## App hooks

- **Lesson content:** animated or illustrated explainers (dopamine flood, then depletion; Pavlov's bell, then your cues; brakes vs. go mode). These are good candidates for short interactive "cards" with quick-check quizzes.
- **Character system:** "Addicted Brain" (tempter) vs. "Rational Brain" (coach). The user's practice "strengthens" the rational brain, which can be visualized as a growing or healing brain map. That directly reflects the book's own "the brain heals" message.
- **Healing timeline:** an abstinence-day visualization that frames time as the brain recovering, using hopeful copy, not medical claims.
- **Medication card** in Resources, with a "talk to a doctor" CTA.

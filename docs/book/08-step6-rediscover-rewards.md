# Ch 8 — Step 6: Rediscover Rewards

Source: epub `index_split_015`. Part 2, Step 6. Paraphrased notes. Based on behavioral activation and behavioral economics.

## Purpose

Fill the void left by alcohol and drugs. Track how time and mood relate, balance "should dos" with "want tos," pick and schedule pleasant activities that **don't trigger you**, solve obstacles, test pessimistic predictions (pleasure predicting), and **reward follow-through**.

## Key concepts

- **Rewards drive everything** (work: money, praise, pride, self-worth). Addiction crowds out *natural* rewards because time goes to getting, using and recovering.
- **Early recovery is often joyless.** Mood swings and anhedonia are common at first (see Ch 2's dopamine depletion). Be patient. Finding new joys takes trial and error.
- **Behavioral economics:** having *access to a range of alternative rewards* predicts quitting. In one study, the **single strongest predictor of staying sober**, regardless of treatment type, was **consistent involvement in a wide range of enjoyable activities** (Farabee, Rawson & McCann 2002). ⇒ *This is the scientific justification for the app's game loop pushing users toward real-world pleasant activities.*
- **Behavioral activation (BA):** from depression treatment. Low mood → fewer pleasant activities → lower mood → more use. BA breaks the cycle and helps with both addiction and depression.
- **Five principles for adding pleasure:**
  1. Pick activities that **won't trigger** cravings.
  2. **Balance** enjoyment with responsibilities.
  3. **Schedule** them.
  4. Put them in your **riskiest times of day**.
  5. Your predictions about enjoyment may be **wrong** (test them).
- **Pleasant activity vs. risky situation.** Before deciding, ask: *"Could this trigger me to want to use?"* Activities, music, venues and companions can all be linked to past use.
- **Should dos vs. want tos.** Too many shoulds feels unfulfilling and brings guilt and dissatisfaction. Too many want-tos with no long-term goals feels empty, like underachieving. Aim for **balance**. It's a lifelong practice.
- **Aim for one pleasant activity per day.** Small is fine. What matters is that you do it *with the intention of enjoying yourself*.
- **Obstacles** can be practical (childcare, the dog, a busy schedule) or **cognitive** ("I don't feel like it," "It won't be fun," "I'll want to go home," "Waste of time," "I'll ruin everyone's fun"). Moods distort expectations, and the activity itself can change your mood.
- **Three-step problem solving:** (1) brainstorm all solutions, (2) weigh the pros and cons and narrow to the best 1–2, (3) try one and evaluate it.
- **What's enjoyable and safe now may differ** from what was enjoyable during active use. Treat it as **discovery**.

## Case story

- **Daniel (marijuana, 5 weeks abstinent):** his friend Holden invites him to see an artist Daniel has only ever seen high. Holden is an old smoking buddy. Craving 8/10. Holden has weed, and Daniel smokes. Better options: **skip it** (safest in early recovery), or **go with a sober friend** and agree on a craving plan (leave early, talk it through).

## Exercises

### Ex 8.0 — "Things I used to enjoy that I did less of" (unnumbered prompt)
- **Type:** free-text list (time with friends or family, exercise, hobbies…).
- **App mapping:** seeds the user's **activity library** as "Rediscover" items.

### Ex 8.1 — Activity Tracking (form)
- **Type:** a daily hourly log, **7 a.m.–10 p.m.**: activity plus **pleasure rating 0–10** for each hour. End of day:
  - **Total pleasant activities** (count rated **≥6**)
  - **Overall mood 0–10**
  - **Peak craving 1–10**
  - **Relapse: yes / no**
- **Guidance:** fill it in **twice a day** (afternoon and evening) for **at least one week**. Don't change anything yet; just observe. Then look for patterns: more pleasant activities → better mood? fewer cravings? Review with a therapist if you have one. (Downloadable form at newharbinger.com/32783.)
- **App mapping:** a **Day Log** screen. Quick-add activity blocks (reusing the Ch 6 Daily Schedule grid), a pleasure slider per block, and an **end-of-day check-in** card (mood, peak craving, used Y/N). Auto-compute the pleasant-activity count. Make two reminder times configurable. Show a **"Mood vs. pleasant activities vs. craving" chart** after 7 days.

### Ex 8.2 — "Should Dos" and "Want Tos"
- **Type:** two lists, up to 10 each. Reflect: which list was harder to fill? Where's the gap?
- **App mapping:** a two-column builder with a **balance meter** visual. Items get tagged *should* or *want* and reused across the planner, so the week view can show the balance.

### Ex 8.3 — Pleasant Activities (menu)
- **Type:** select from a menu of about 60 everyday activities (walks, cooking, reading, museums, zoo, dance class, movies, friends, dates, trips, parties, bike rides, library, exercise, calling someone, dinner guests, eating out, gardening, drawing, art projects, music, sports, TV, dessert, hiking, flowers, decorating, writing, theater, a haircut, a bath, religious activities, singing, parks, an instrument, meditating, family, massage, organizing photos, internet, video calls, a takeout movie night, the beach, poetry, journaling, making gifts, an aquarium, skating, puzzles, jewelry, the mall, a class, pets, bowling, volunteering, photography). Plus up to 6 of your own.
- **App mapping:** a **pleasant-activity library** (cards with category, cost, solo or social, time needed). Each user flags each activity as **"risky for me?"** (connecting to the Trigger Profile). Write the starter list in our own words and organize it into categories.

### Ex 8.4 — Activity Scheduling
- **Type:** up to 10 activities, each with a planned date. Keep tracking to monitor the should/want balance.
- **App mapping:** drag activities from the library onto the planner. The planner suggests **risky time slots** (from the Ch 6 schedule and craving-log analytics) as the best places to put them.

### Ex 8.4b — Obstacles and Problem Solving (unnumbered)
- **Type:** list obstacles (up to 4). For each: **brainstorm** up to 5 solutions → **pros and cons table** → pick the **best 2** → try one → **evaluate**.
- **App mapping:** a reusable **Problem Solver** tool (also useful for Ch 9 stressors). Attachable to any planned activity that keeps getting skipped.

### Ex 8.5 / 8.6 — Pleasure Predicting (and form)
- **Type:** per activity: **predicted enjoyment 0–100%** (+ thoughts) → **do it, even if you predicted 0** → thoughts during → **actual enjoyment 0–100%** → compare.
- **App mapping:** a **"Prediction vs. Reality" experiment**. Before an activity, set a predicted-enjoyment slider. Afterward, a prompt for the actual rating. Over time, chart **predicted vs. actual**, which usually shows the user underestimates. This is a natural **"science experiment" game mechanic**: a "Mood Lies" discovery badge when actual > predicted.

### Ex 8.7 — Reward Yourself for Scheduling and Following Through
- **Type:** weekly, 3 entries: "I will [activity] on [date], and I will reward myself by [reward]."
- **App mapping:** the planner plus **self-chosen real-world rewards** (shared with the Ch 4 exercise log). The app confirms follow-through and prompts the user to actually enjoy the reward ("You earned: bubble bath"). The book says to link the reward explicitly to the behavior.

## App hooks

- **The "Want To" engine** is the heart of the "positive" side of the app. Pleasant activities are first-class tasks in the Actions tracker.
- **Risk check on every activity** ("Could this trigger me?"), with optional suggestions for a sober buddy and an exit plan.
- **Analytics:** pleasant-activity count vs. mood vs. craving. This is the self-expert data loop.
- **Gamification fit:** XP for doing pleasant activities (not only for completing lessons), and pleasure-prediction experiments as "quests." The game should *point outward to real life*, not become the user's new reward source.
